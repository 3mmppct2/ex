# 3. Topic 6 — Energetics

## 3.1 Specification Requirements

### 6.1 Enthalpy Change

Understand that:
- Enthalpy change, ΔH, represents heat energy change at constant pressure.
- Standard pressure is 100 kPa.
- Standard temperature is specified, usually 298 K.

### 6.2 Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

- Exothermic: ΔH < 0
- Endothermic: ΔH > 0

### 6.3 Enthalpy Level Diagrams

Construct and interpret diagrams showing:
- Reactant and product energy levels
- Exothermic and endothermic changes
- Correct direction and sign of ΔH

### 6.4 Standard Enthalpy Changes

Know precise definitions of:
- Standard enthalpy change of reaction, ΔrH
- Standard enthalpy change of formation, ΔfH
- Standard enthalpy change of combustion, ΔcH
- Standard enthalpy change of neutralisation, ΔneutH
- Standard enthalpy change of atomisation, ΔatH

Definitions must correctly specify:
- The chemical process
- The amount of substance involved
- Standard conditions
- Standard states where relevant

### 6.5 Calorimetry

Use:

q = mcΔT

Where:
- q = heat energy transferred, J
- m = mass, g
- c = specific heat capacity, J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹
- ΔT = temperature change, °C

Calculate molar enthalpy changes in kJ mol⁻¹.

Relevant experimental contexts:
- Reactions in insulated containers
- Combustion calorimetry

Account for the sign of the enthalpy change.

### 6.6 Hess's Law

Know and apply Hess's Law.

Be able to:
- Construct enthalpy cycles
- Calculate unknown enthalpy changes
- Reverse equations and change signs correctly
- Multiply enthalpy changes when equations are multiplied
- Use experimental and tabulated data

### 6.7 Core Practical 2

Determination of the enthalpy change of a reaction using Hess's Law.

### 6.8 Experimental Evaluation

Evaluate:
- Heat loss to surroundings
- Heat absorption by apparatus
- Measurement uncertainty
- Assumptions in calorimetry
- Cooling curve corrections
- Systematic errors
- Limitations of combustion calorimetry

### 6.9 Bond Enthalpy

Understand:
- Bond enthalpy
- Mean bond enthalpy
- Limitations of mean bond enthalpy data

Use:

ΔH ≈ Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken)
     − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed)

Breaking bonds is endothermic.

Forming bonds is exothermic.

### 6.10 Mean Bond Enthalpy Calculations

Calculate unknown mean bond enthalpies from reaction enthalpy data.

### 6.11 Bond Strength and Reactivity

Understand how bond enthalpy data may indicate:
- Which bond is more easily broken
- Relative difficulty of bond breaking
- Possible implications for reaction rate

# 4. Topic 7 — Intermolecular Forces

## 4.1 Specification Requirements

### 7.1 Types of Intermolecular Forces

Understand:
- London forces
- Permanent dipole–permanent dipole interactions
- Hydrogen bonding

London forces arise from instantaneous dipole-induced dipole interactions.

### 7.2 Hydrogen Bonding

Understand hydrogen bonding in:
- H₂O
- NH₃
- HF

Explain hydrogen bonding using:
- Bond polarity
- Appropriate electronegative atoms
- Lone pairs of electrons
- Intermolecular attraction

### 7.3 Properties of Water

Explain:
- Relatively high melting temperature
- Relatively high boiling temperature
- Lower density of ice compared with liquid water

Link these properties to hydrogen bonding.

### 7.4 Predicting Hydrogen Bonding

Predict whether unfamiliar analogous molecules can form hydrogen bonds.

### 7.5 Physical Properties

Explain:
- Increasing boiling temperatures of alkanes with chain length
- Effect of branching on boiling temperatures
- Higher boiling temperatures of alcohols compared with similar alkanes
- Boiling temperature trends in hydrogen halides from HF to HI

Use:
- Number of electrons
- Strength of London forces
- Molecular shape and contact surface area
- Hydrogen bonding where appropriate

### 7.6 Solubility

Explain solvent choice in terms of intermolecular interactions.

Relevant cases:
- Dissolution of ionic compounds in water through hydration
- Dissolution of simple alcohols in water through hydrogen bonding
- Poor water solubility of compounds unable to form sufficient hydrogen bonds
- Solubility in non-aqueous solvents with similar intermolecular forces

Oxidising agent:
- Gains electrons
- Is reduced

Reducing agent:
- Loses electrons

# 6. Topic 9 — Introduction to Kinetics and Equilibria

## 6.1 Kinetics

### 9.1 Collision Theory

Explain the effects of:
- Concentration
- Temperature
- Pressure
- Surface area

Use collision frequency and successful collisions appropriately.

### 9.2 Activation Energy

Understand that successful reactions require collisions with sufficient energy.

### 9.3 Reaction Rate Calculations

Calculate reaction rates using:

Rate = 1 / time

Or gradients of graphs.

For instantaneous or initial rates, use the gradient of a suitable tangent.

### 9.4 Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

Explain how temperature changes:
- Molecular energy distribution
- Proportion of particles with energy ≥ Ea
- Frequency of successful collisions

### 9.5 Catalysts

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy.

### 9.6 Reaction Profiles

Draw reaction profiles showing:
- Uncatalysed pathway
- Catalysed pathway
- Activation energies
- Relevant intermediate energy levels

### 9.7 Industrial Catalysts

Understand sustainability benefits, including:
- Reduced energy requirements
- Improvements in atom economy where applicable

### 9.8 Catalyst and Maxwell–Boltzmann Explanations

Explain the effect of lower activation energy on the proportion of particles able to react.


## 6.2 Equilibria

### 9.9 Dynamic Equilibrium

At dynamic equilibrium:
- Forward and reverse reactions continue.
- Their rates are equal.
- Reactant and product concentrations remain constant.

Equal concentrations are not required.

### 9.10 Le Chatelier's Principle

Predict and justify the effects of:
- Temperature
- Pressure
- Concentration

on homogeneous equilibria.

### 9.11 Industrial Conditions

Evaluate compromises between:
- Equilibrium yield
- Reaction rate
- Temperature
- Pressure

Use the reaction equation and supplied information.

## Common Conceptual Distinctions Illustrated

Energetics:
- Use the correct amount of limiting reagent.
- Distinguish enthalpy of vaporisation from condensation.
- Account for the sign of enthalpy change.

Uncertainty:
- A titre is calculated from two burette readings.
- Both readings contribute to uncertainty.
- Distinguish absolute and percentage uncertainty.

Intermolecular forces:
- Identify whether a molecule can form intermolecular hydrogen bonds.

Group chemistry:
- Identify which solvent layer contains a dissolved halogen.
- Apply oxidation numbers correctly.

Kinetics:
- Greater surface area can increase reaction rate without changing the total amount of product when the limiting reagent is unchanged.
- Higher temperature increases the proportion of particles with energy above the activation energy.
- Changing concentration does not, by itself, change the proportion of particles above Ea at constant temperature.

Equilibrium:
- At dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal.
- A catalyst increases both forward and reverse reaction rates.
- A catalyst does not change the equilibrium position.

Spectroscopy:
- Use molecular ion and fragment peaks together.

Halogenoalkanes:
- Carbon–halogen bond strength affects hydrolysis rate.
- Silver bromide is a cream precipitate.
- Silver bromide is insoluble in dilute aqueous ammonia.
- Hydrolysis is a nucleophilic substitution reaction.

## Question 14(a) — Catalysts and Activation Energy

3 marks.

Required points:

1. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.
2. More particles have sufficient energy to react.
3. The frequency of successful collisions increases.

Additional guidance:
- An appropriate diagram can support the activation energy point.
- The exact wording concerning collision frequency matters.
- The mark scheme ignores the phrase "rate of successful collisions" for the third point.

Preferred explanation:

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of particles have sufficient energy to react and the frequency of successful collisions increases.


## Question 14(b)(i) — Intermolecular Forces

4 marks.

Required points:

1. The hydrides have London forces.
2. London forces become stronger down the group as the number of electrons increases.
3. Ammonia has hydrogen bonding.
4. Hydrogen bonding in ammonia is stronger than its London forces.

Accepted terminology:
- London forces
- London dispersion forces
- van der Waals forces
- Instantaneous dipole-induced dipole interactions

Important restrictions:
- Do not claim that other hydrides in the comparison have hydrogen bonding.
- Do not claim London forces remain similar down the group.
- Unnecessary dipole–dipole discussion receives no additional credit.


## Question 14(b)(ii) — Hydrogen as a Fuel

3 marks.

1. Hydrogen combustion produces water.
2. Alkane combustion produces carbon dioxide and water.
3. Carbon dioxide contributes to the enhanced greenhouse effect/global warming.

Additional guidance:
- Equations may support relevant marking points.

## Question 14(c)(ii) — Nucleophilic Substitution Mechanism

3 marks.

Six indicative mechanism points:

1. Correct C–Br bond dipole.
2. Curly arrow from the nitrogen lone pair of ammonia to the carbon attached to bromine.
3. Curly arrow from the C–Br bond towards bromine.
4. Curly arrow showing N–H bond electron movement during deprotonation.
5. Curly arrow from an ammonia lone pair towards the relevant hydrogen.
6. Correct ammonium and bromide products, or NH₄Br.

Mark allocation:
- 6 points = 3 marks
- 4–5 points = 2 marks
- 2–3 points = 1 mark

Important restrictions:
- Curly arrows must originate from appropriate electron pairs or bonds.
- Charges must be correct.
- Fish-hook arrows are inappropriate for this electron-pair mechanism.

A written explanation alone may not reproduce every visual requirement of the mechanism.

Unstructured points without meaningful logical connections.

# 13. Summer 2025 Mark Scheme — Section C

Section C total: 21 marks.

## Question 17(a) — Titration and Hydrated Salt Calculation

7 marks.

The calculation involves determining the value of n in:

MgCO₃·nH₂O

Marking stages:

1. Moles of NaOH in the titre.
2. Total moles of remaining HCl.
3. Moles of HCl reacting with hydrated magnesium carbonate.
4. Moles of hydrated magnesium carbonate.
5. Mass of anhydrous magnesium carbonate.
6. Mass of water.
7. Mole ratio and final value of n.

Example working:

n(NaOH)
= (27.15 ÷ 1000) × 0.0960
= 0.0026064 mol

Total remaining HCl:

0.0026064 × 10
= 0.026064 mol

HCl consumed:

0.0600 − 0.026064
= 0.033936 mol

Moles MgCO₃·nH₂O:

0.033936 ÷ 2
= 0.016968 mol

Mass MgCO₃:

0.016968 × (24.3 + 12.0 + 3 × 16.0)
= 1.4304 g

Mass H₂O:

2.35 − 1.4304
= 0.9196 g

Mole ratio:

n = (0.9196 ÷ 18.0) ÷ 0.016968
n = 3.01

Final answer:

n = 3

Marking notes:
- Follow-through marks are permitted.
- Alternative mathematically correct methods are accepted.
- Correct intermediate chemical ratios are important.


## Question 17(b)(i) — Calorimetry

3 marks.

Example:

q = mcΔT

q = 40 × 4.18 × 30.8
q = 5149.8 J

Moles MgO:

n = 1.92 ÷ (24.3 + 16.0)
n = 0.047643 mol

Enthalpy change:

ΔrH = −(5149.8 ÷ 1000) ÷ 0.047643

ΔrH = −108.1 kJ mol⁻¹

Required:
- Correct heat energy
- Correct moles
- Correct enthalpy value, sign and units

A positive sign in this example loses the sign-dependent mark.

The official mark scheme allows full marks for the correct final answer without working, but showing working is preferable for reliability.


## Question 17(b)(ii) — Hess Cycle

3 marks.

Required:
1. Correct completed Hess cycle.
2. Correct algebraic relationship.
3. Correct enthalpy result.

Example:

ΔrH = ΔrH₂ − ΔrH₁

ΔrH = 3.58 − (−108.09)

ΔrH = +111.67 kJ mol⁻¹

Final:

ΔrH = +112 kJ mol⁻¹

Follow-through from the preceding part is permitted.


## Question 17(b)(iii) — Experimental Limitation

1 mark.

Required principle:

It is difficult to measure the temperature of a solid or powder while it is being heated.

Generic discussion of non-standard conditions does not address the specified limitation.


## Question 17(c)(i) — Group 2 Thermal Stability

3 marks.

Required points:

1. Calcium carbonate is more thermally stable than magnesium carbonate.
2. Ca²⁺ is larger than Mg²⁺ and has lower polarising power.
3. The carbonate ion is less polarised, so the C–O bonds are less weakened.

Preferred linked explanation:

Calcium carbonate is more thermally stable because Ca²⁺ has a larger ionic radius and lower charge density than Mg²⁺. It therefore has less polarising power, so the carbonate ion is less distorted and its C–O bonds are less weakened.

Important:
- Use ionic size and polarising power.
- Do not incorrectly explain the trend using intermolecular forces.


## Question 17(c)(ii) — Industrial Equilibrium Conditions

4 marks.

Required points:

1. The forward reaction is exothermic, so lower temperature favours methanol production.
2. At lower temperature, the reaction rate is too slow, requiring a compromise temperature.
3. The product side contains fewer moles of gas.
4. Higher pressure favours the product side and increases methanol yield.

Additional guidance:
- Reverse arguments may be accepted.
- Relevant statements about increased collision frequency under higher pressure may receive credit.
- Unnecessary catalyst discussion is ignored for the specified marking points.

Preferred linked explanation:

The forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature shifts equilibrium towards methanol and increases yield. However, a lower temperature reduces reaction rate, so a compromise temperature is required. There are fewer moles of gas on the product side, so higher pressure shifts equilibrium towards methanol and increases yield.

# 14. Calculation and Numerical Answer Guidance

## 14.1 General Principles

For calculations:

1. Identify the required relationship.
2. Substitute values with correct units.
3. Apply the relevant mole ratio.
4. Perform unit conversions.
5. Retain sufficient precision during intermediate calculations.
6. Give the final value with appropriate significant figures.
7. Include the correct units.
8. Verify signs where applicable.

## 14.2 Core Unit 2 Relationships

Moles:

n = m / M

Solution concentration:

c = n / V

Where V is in dm³.

Calorimetry:

q = mcΔT

Molar enthalpy:

ΔH = −q / n

Use the sign appropriate to the reaction and the definition of q.

Bond enthalpy:

ΔH ≈ ΣE(bonds broken) − ΣE(bonds formed)

Reaction rate:

Rate = 1 / time

Percentage uncertainty:

Percentage uncertainty
= (absolute uncertainty / measured value) × 100

## 14.3 Titration Calculations

Identify:
- Balanced equation
- Known concentration
- Titre
- Mole ratio
- Unknown amount or concentration

When appropriate:
- Select concordant titres.
- Calculate the mean titre.
- Convert cm³ to dm³.
- Use mole ratios from the balanced equation.

## 14.4 Uncertainty

When a titre requires two burette readings, account for the uncertainty of both readings.

Distinguish:
- Absolute uncertainty
- Percentage uncertainty
- Random error
- Systematic error

Do not assume all experimental errors affect results in the same direction.

## 14.5 Error Carried Forward

Official mark schemes may permit follow-through credit.

An incorrect intermediate answer may still earn later method marks if used consistently and correctly.

Always verify:
- Stoichiometric ratios
- Signs
- Units
- Final rounding

# 17. Kinetics and Equilibrium Answer Requirements

- Sufficient energy

→ alternative reaction pathway
→ lower activation energy

→ increased frequency of successful collisions

## 17.2 Equilibrium

Always distinguish:
- Reaction rate
- Equilibrium position
- Equilibrium yield

For temperature:
- Identify the exothermic or endothermic direction.

- Consider the effect on reaction rate where relevant.

For pressure:
- Compare gaseous mole numbers.
- Identify the favoured side.
- State the resulting effect on equilibrium yield.

For catalysts:
- Both forward and reverse reaction rates increase.
- Equilibrium position does not change.

1. Prioritise the exact chemical requirements of the question.

2. Use precise Pearson Edexcel chemistry terminology.

3. Include enough distinct marking points for the available marks.

5. Use correct reagents, conditions, products and observations.

6. Distinguish chemical species carefully.

7. Balance chemical and ionic equations.

8. Use correct oxidation numbers and charges.

9. Show relevant mathematical working.

10. Include correct signs, units and significant figures.

11. Follow the requested chemical representation.

12. Use information from the question's diagrams, tables, graphs and spectra.

13. For practical questions, provide specific experimental details.

14. For evaluation questions, explain how limitations affect the reliability, accuracy or validity of results.

18. When the exact official mark scheme is available, use its marking points and additional guidance.

20. Aim for the shortest chemically complete response capable of securing the available marks.
